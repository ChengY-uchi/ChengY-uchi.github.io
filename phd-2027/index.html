Statement of purpose

My first research asked what a person’s gait reveals about how they feel. I developed a model of how different parts of the body move together over time to recognize emotion from movement. When I turned to 3D reconstruction, I began asking a related question in reverse. Given images of an object at rest, could I represent it well enough to predict what would happen if someone pushed or pulled it?

I pursued that question in PowerFlex. I developed a way to edit reconstructed objects that accounts for how their parts are connected and how stiff or flexible their materials are. This allowed me to change the stiffness of a part and study how the same pull produced a different deformation. In PAVE, I studied how to retain that control when generating a video. If I added something to a scene, I wanted it to stay where I put it as the camera moved. I used an edited image to specify what should change and anchored it in 3D to guide the generation of subsequent views.

In robotics, I became interested in how an agent recognizes that an intended change has actually happened. A robot following a demonstration can perform the individual movements and still reach for the next object before it has finished placing the first. In Decoupled Reading, I separated the problem of finding the relevant moment in a demonstration from that of executing the movement. In WAIT, I trained the policy to judge from its own observations when a step was complete. This improved execution on longer tasks across two policies and in real-robot trials.

For my PhD, I want to bring physical prediction and skill composition together. My work on imitation has assumed that someone can demonstrate the whole sequence. I want to study how a robot could instead draw on skills learned on separate occasions, predict whether the outcome of one will make the next possible, and revise its plan when an object behaves differently than expected. I would judge such a model by whether the robot can carry out a new combination of skills and recover when an intermediate step goes wrong.
